Trading Glossary: 40 Essential Terms Every Beginner Must Know

← Back to all articles

Trading has a language, and beginners who do not speak it cannot evaluate strategies, read broker documents or follow market commentary. This glossary covers the 40 terms you will encounter constantly — in plain English, with Indian market context where it matters.

Bookmark it and return whenever a new term appears.

Market basics

Bid/ask: the highest buy and lowest sell prices; the spread between them is a transaction cost. Volume: shares/contracts traded — confirms price moves. Liquidity: how easily you can trade without moving the price; illiquid stocks trap traders. Market/limit orders: execute now at any price vs execute only at your price or better. Slippage: the gap between expected and actual fill price, worst in fast markets.

Circuit limits: Indian exchanges halt or band stocks after large moves — a key difference from forex. STT, stamp duty, GST: the statutory costs on every Indian trade; know them before calculating 'profits'.

Positions and risk language

Long/short: profiting from rises/falls. Leverage/margin: borrowed exposure — see our leverage guide. Stop-loss: the pre-planned exit that caps loss. Risk-reward ratio: potential gain vs potential loss per trade. Drawdown: peak-to-trough account decline. Position sizing: how much to trade — the subject of our sizing guide.

R-multiple: profit or loss measured in units of initial risk — the professional's unit of account.

Derivatives and analysis terms

Futures/options: contracts to buy/sell later — futures obligate, options give the right. Strike price, premium, expiry, intrinsic/time value: the options vocabulary. Lot size: the standardised contract quantity on NSE/BSE. Open interest: outstanding derivative contracts — a sentiment gauge.

Support/resistance, trend, breakout, pullback, moving average, RSI: the technical analysis core — each covered in our strategy guides. Backtesting: testing a strategy on historical data. Paper trading: practising without real money.

Order types glossary: the execution vocabulary

Beyond market and limit orders lies the execution vocabulary professionals use daily. Stop-loss orders (SL) trigger when price hits your level and execute as limit orders; stop-loss market orders (SL-M) trigger and execute immediately — guaranteed fill with possible slippage. GTT (Good Till Triggered) orders persist across sessions until triggered, essential for swing traders. IOC (Immediate or Cancel) orders fill what they can instantly and cancel the rest; FOK (Fill or Kill) demands complete immediate fill or nothing. Cover and bracket orders (where offered) bundle entry with built-in stop and target. Each type exists for a specific situation; using only market orders is like owning one club for the whole golf course.

Learn these in your broker's platform specifically, because implementations and availability vary: place each order type in a simulator or with tiny quantities, observe exactly how triggers, modifications and cancellations behave, and note which types persist overnight. Pay special attention to how your broker handles stop-loss orders in fast markets and what their auto-square-off cutoffs do to intraday GTTs. Execution fluency — knowing without thinking which order type each situation demands — is an underrated trading skill. It never makes headlines, but in volatile moments it is the difference between controlled exits and costly surprises.

Frequently asked questions

What is the bid-ask spread?

The difference between the highest price buyers will pay (bid) and the lowest price sellers will accept (ask). It is effectively a cost of trading — narrower spreads in liquid instruments mean cheaper trading.

What does R-multiple mean?

Your profit or loss expressed in units of the risk you took. Risking ₹5,000 to make ₹10,000 is +2R; losing ₹5,000 is −1R. Professionals evaluate strategies in R, not rupees.

What is slippage?

The difference between the price you expected when placing an order and the price you actually got — common in fast markets and illiquid instruments, and a real cost most beginners underestimate.
