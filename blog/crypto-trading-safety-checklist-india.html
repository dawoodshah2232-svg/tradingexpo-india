Crypto Trading Safety Checklist: 10 Steps Before You Buy in India

Trading Expo India editorial team·September 24, 2026·3 min read

← Back to all articles

Crypto gives you something equity markets never do: total personal responsibility for security. There is no broker to reverse a mistaken transfer and no regulator to recover coins sent to a scammer. Your safety setup is your only safety net.

Run through this ten-step checklist before your next buy — most of it takes under an hour.

Platform and account hardening

One: use an FIU-IND-registered exchange that complies with Indian regulations. Two: enable two-factor authentication with an authenticator app, not SMS — SIM-swap attacks are a real and growing threat in India. Three: set withdrawal allowlists so funds can only go to your own addresses, and enable anti-phishing codes so fake exchange emails are easy to spot.

Four: use a unique, strong password managed by a password manager, and a dedicated email address for crypto accounts. Credential-stuffing attacks harvest logins from breached sites and try them on exchanges automatically.

Custody: not your keys, not your coins

Five: keep only active trading capital on exchanges; move long-term holdings to self-custody — a reputable hardware wallet for meaningful sums. Six: back up seed phrases on paper or metal, stored in two separate secure locations, and never photograph or cloud-store them.

Seven: test with a tiny transaction before any large transfer, and always verify the full destination address — clipboard-hijacking malware swaps addresses at paste time. One test transaction has saved countless traders from catastrophic typos.

Scam immunity

Eight: assume every DM offering help, giveaways or 'support' is a scam — real exchanges never ask for your seed phrase or remote access. Nine: be sceptical of tokens you did not buy appearing in your wallet; interacting with malicious airdrops can drain connected wallets.

Ten: remember the taxman sees everything on compliant exchanges, so keep records as you go — our crypto tax guide explains why. Safety and compliance are the same habit.

When things go wrong: the incident response playbook

Prevention is ideal; response is essential, because even careful traders get targeted. If you suspect a breach: one, immediately transfer remaining funds to a clean wallet or account from a secure device — speed matters more than forensics. Two, revoke token approvals and active sessions on the compromised platform. Three, change passwords and rotate 2FA from a device you trust, and check for email forwarding rules attackers commonly plant. Four, document everything with timestamps and screenshots before memories fade. Five, file a cybercrime complaint at cybercrime.gov.in and inform your bank if fiat rails were involved — reports within hours have meaningfully better outcomes than reports within weeks.

After the incident, conduct a blameless post-mortem: how did the attacker get in — phishing, SIM-swap, malware, or a leaked seed phrase? Close that specific vector before resuming any activity, and upgrade the corresponding control (hardware key instead of SMS 2FA, for example). Share anonymised lessons with trading communities you trust; the attack that hit you is being run against thousands of others. Finally, resist the urge to 'trade your way back' to recover losses — incident recovery and trading recovery are separate processes, and mixing them is how a security loss becomes a trading blowup too.

Frequently asked questions

Is SMS 2FA safe for crypto exchanges?

It is better than nothing but vulnerable to SIM-swap attacks, where criminals take over your number. Authenticator apps or hardware keys are significantly safer.

Should I keep crypto on the exchange?

Keep only what you actively trade on exchanges. Long-term holdings are safer in self-custody (hardware wallets), since exchange balances depend on the platform's solvency and security.

What is a withdrawal allowlist?

A security setting that restricts withdrawals to pre-approved addresses, usually with a time delay for adding new ones. It means a hacker who breaches your account still cannot send funds to their own wallet.
