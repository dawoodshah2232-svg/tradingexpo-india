The Great Indian Flow Split: FPIs Are Selling the Market and Buying the IPOs

Trading Expo India editorial team·September 28, 2026·6 min read

← Back to all articles

Foreign investors are not leaving India. They are rearranging it. In September so far, FPIs sold ₹25,682 crore of Indian equities through the exchanges — and simultaneously put ₹8,551 crore into the primary market, through IPOs and fresh issuances. The net equity outflow for the month stands near ₹17,131 crore, but the headline number hides the real story: the same investors dumping listed shares are the ones bidding for new ones.

This is not a new habit. It is the pattern of 2026. So far this year, FPI selling through the exchanges has totalled a staggering ₹2,95,971 crore, while investment through the primary market has reached ₹54,398 crore. Money is not fleeing India; it is rotating from the listed past into the unlisted future — and leaving a trail of red candles behind it.

₹25,682 crore sold through exchanges in September. ₹8,551 crore invested through IPOs in the same month. ₹2.96 lakh crore of exchange selling for the year — against ₹54,398 crore of primary-market buying.

Seven weeks of red

The selling has a visible price. Indian benchmarks closed lower for the seventh consecutive week — the Sensex down 0.54% to 73,895.74 and the Nifty down 0.88% to 23,140.50 for the week, with both midcap and smallcap indices also under pressure. Technology and financials were among the week's key laggards; realty emerged as the strongest-performing segment.

September is a sharp reversal from the positive FPI inflows of July and August — the third such episode of heavy selling this year, each one triggered by a surge in global risk aversion tied to energy prices or US monetary tightening. The message to traders: the domestic economy can be resilient while the market bleeds, because the driver is external.

Why the money is going out — and where it goes instead

Three forces are pulling capital away from listed Indian equities, and every trader should know them by name.

First, US yields. The US 10-year bond is near 5%, and the Federal Reserve just delivered a 25-basis-point rate hike, tightening global liquidity further. When dollar bonds pay this well, emerging-market equities look expensive by comparison — and risk managers at foreign funds are paid to notice. This is the single biggest reason the FPI exodus is expected to continue, according to analysts.

Second, crude and conflict. Elevated crude oil prices, wrapped in the uncertainty of the ongoing Iran-US conflict, are a double hit for India: they squeeze the economy's import bill and spook global risk appetite. As long as crude stays high and geopolitics stay hot, FPI flows will stay nervous.

Third, the IPO mirage. India's primary market keeps delivering, and FPIs are following the returns. Selling listed shares while buying new issuances is not a contradiction — it is a preference. Fresh listings come with pricing discounts and momentum; stale listed large-caps come with global headwinds. Analysts note that FPI money is "chasing the market momentum," which is why they have been sustained buyers in mid- and small-caps even as they dump large-caps.

The large-cap / small-cap split

Perhaps the most useful detail for traders sits inside the flow data itself. Dr. V K Vijayakumar, Chief Investment Strategist at Geojit Investments Ltd, highlights a notable divergence: FPIs are sellers in large-caps but have remained sustained buyers in mid- and small-cap stocks. They are not just derisking — they are stock-picking by segment.

This matters for how you trade the tape. Nifty is a large-cap index, so persistent FPI selling keeps the benchmark heavy even when the domestic story looks fine. Mid- and small-caps have pockets of support from the same foreign hands — until momentum breaks, at which point the selling has further to run. If you trade index options, understand that your underlyings carry a structural seller. If you trade midcap cash, understand that your support has momentum behind it — and momentum is a fickle friend.

Debt is bleeding too

Equities are not the only exit. FPIs also sold debt in September so far: ₹7,385 crore through the Fully Accessible Route (FAR), ₹3,021 crore through the Voluntary Retention Route, and ₹1,889 crore through the General Limit route, per depository data. This is broad de-risking of India exposure, not a rotation within it — which is why the rupee, rates, and equities are all worth watching together.

What traders should actually do with this

Flow data is not just colour for business channels — it is tradable context. Four practical takeaways:

  • Respect the structural seller in large-caps. As long as US yields sit near 5% and the Fed is tightening, expect rallies in Nifty and Bank Nifty to be sold. Fade breakouts with wide stops, or wait for price action to confirm before chasing.
  • Watch mid/small-cap momentum, but use trailing stops. The segment FPIs are buying can keep outperforming — and can snap when momentum turns. If you trade midcaps, position size as if the bid can vanish.
  • Volatility is likely to stay elevated. Analysts expect chop until there is clarity on crude oil prices and geopolitics. That suits intraday option sellers on expiry days and punishes overnight leverage.
  • Track the flows yourself. NSDL publishes provisional FPI data daily, and the primary-vs-secondary split tells you more than the net number. The day the exchange-selling number shrinks is your early signal that the headwind is fading.

The great flow split will end the way every FPI episode ends: yields fall, crude cools, or valuations get too cheap for the momentum chasers to resist. Until then, the market's message is consistent — and worth trading with, not against.

Frequently asked questions

How much did FPIs sell in September 2026?

FPIs sold ₹25,682 crore of Indian equities through the exchanges in September (through 25 September), while investing ₹8,551 crore through the primary market — leaving net equity outflows of about ₹17,131 crore for the month so far, per depository data.

Why are FPIs selling Indian equities?

High US bond yields (the US 10-year near 5%), a 25-basis-point Fed rate hike, elevated crude oil prices and uncertainty around the Iran-US conflict are pulling global capital toward safer dollar assets and away from emerging-market equities.

Why are FPIs investing in Indian IPOs while selling listed shares?

Foreign investors have shifted from selling listed large-caps to selectively buying new issuances, where India's IPO market has offered better returns. They remain sellers in large-caps but sustained buyers in mid- and small-caps, chasing momentum in segments that keep performing.
