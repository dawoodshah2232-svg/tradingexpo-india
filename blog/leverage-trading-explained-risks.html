Leverage in Trading Explained: The Double-Edged Sword

Trading Expo India editorial team·September 24, 2026·3 min read

← Back to all articles

Leverage — controlling a large position with a small deposit — is simultaneously trading's greatest attraction and its greatest destroyer. Brokers advertise 10x, 50x, 100x leverage as opportunity; risk managers describe the same numbers as the speed at which accounts die.

Understand the mechanics and the maths before you touch it.

How leverage actually works

With 10x leverage, ₹10,000 margin controls a ₹1,00,000 position. A 1% favourable move doubles your margin (+₹1,000 = +10%); a 1% adverse move halves it. At 100x, a 1% move against you wipes the position entirely — the margin call or auto-square-off arrives before you can react.

Leverage does not change the market's movement; it changes your sensitivity to it. Small normal fluctuations become account-level events.

The maths of ruin

Consider 50x leverage: a 2% adverse move — utterly routine intraday noise — destroys the position. To survive, your timing must be near-perfect, repeatedly. Now consider that even professional traders are wrong 40–50% of the time. High leverage converts normal win rates into certain ruin through the mathematics of repeated exposure.

This is why leverage kills beginners specifically: they lack the timing skill that high leverage demands, while high leverage denies them the time to develop it.

Five rules for surviving leverage

One: use the minimum leverage your strategy needs — most successful traders use far less than available. Two: size positions by risk (1% rule), letting leverage serve the stop distance, not your ambition. Three: never add to leveraged losers. Four: avoid maximum leverage around news events, when slippage explodes. Five: if you cannot explain your liquidation price before entering, do not enter.

Leverage is a tool for precise, planned risk — not a lottery ticket multiplier.

Margin systems in India: SPAN, VAR and what brokers charge

Indian margin collection is more structured than the 'leverage' marketing suggests. For derivatives, SPAN margins — calculated from the exchange's risk arrays — set the baseline, covering potential one-day losses at high confidence levels; brokers add exposure margins on top. For equities, VAR (Value at Risk) based margins apply. These are regulatory floors, not broker choices: when your broker demands a certain margin, much of it is the exchange's requirement passed through. Understanding this reframes leverage from 'my broker gives me 10x' to 'the system requires this much collateral for this much risk' — a healthier mental model.

On top of exchange margins, brokers set their own policies: intraday leverage offered (within regulatory limits), interest charged on funded debit balances, and charges for margin shortfalls including penalties the exchange levies and brokers pass on. Peak-margin reporting rules mean shortfalls are computed on the highest margin utilisation of the day — intraday spikes count, not just end-of-day positions. The practical takeaways: maintain buffer capital above minimum margins, understand your broker's square-off timings and charges, and never treat 'available margin' as 'safe to deploy fully'. Margin is the system's safety equipment; use it with the respect its designers intended.

Frequently asked questions

What is leverage in trading?

Using borrowed funds or margin to control a larger position than your capital alone allows. 10x leverage means ₹10,000 controls ₹1,00,000 of exposure — amplifying both profits and losses tenfold.

Why is high leverage dangerous?

Because normal market noise becomes fatal: at 100x leverage a 1% adverse move wipes out the position. It demands near-perfect timing while eliminating the room for error beginners need to learn.

How much leverage should a beginner use?

As little as possible — many professionals trade with effective leverage under 3–5x. Master unleveraged or low-leverage trading first; add leverage only when strategy and discipline are proven.
