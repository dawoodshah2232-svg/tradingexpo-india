Jane Street vs SEBI at SAT: The Bank Nifty Expiry Fight — and Why Indian F&O Traders Should Pay Attention

Trading Expo India editorial team·October 6, 2026·7 min read

← Back to all articles

One of the largest enforcement battles in Indian market history returned to the Securities Appellate Tribunal on Monday, 5 October 2026 — and the defence came out swinging. Lawyers for Jane Street, the New York trading powerhouse, denied the market manipulation charges sitting behind SEBI's ₹4,843-crore impound order, and argued that the regulator's own surveillance machinery had once found no evidence of wrongdoing. The next hearing was scheduled for 6 October — today.

For Indian traders, this is not a faraway courtroom drama between a Wall Street firm and a regulator. The case is about expiry-day trading in the Bank Nifty and Nifty — the exact days when millions of Indian retail traders place their options bets. Whatever the tribunal decides will shape how India's derivatives market is policed for years.

The case so far: ₹4,843 crore and 18 expiry days

The dispute traces back to an interim order SEBI passed on 3 July 2025. The regulator alleged that Jane Street and related entities had manipulated the prices of the Nifty 50 and Bank Nifty indices through coordinated trading in the cash and derivatives markets, enabling the group to make unlawful gains. SEBI's order examined 18 key trading days on which it alleged manipulation of the Bank Nifty, covering the period from January 2023 to March 2025, and also probed three expiry days in May 2025 for alleged manipulation involving the Nifty.

The regulator ordered the impounding of about ₹4,843 crore (roughly $503 million) of alleged unlawful gains. Jane Street denied the allegations, challenged the order before the SAT, and deposited the impounded amount — but has not resumed trading in Indian markets. In September 2025, the tribunal directed SEBI to respond to the appeal within three weeks, and the matter has been moving through hearings since, with a hearing fixed for 5 October 2026.

The defence: “the index moved the other way”

At the 5 October hearing, Jane Street's counsel, Darius Khambata, put the firm's case bluntly. Central to the defence is data from earlier surveillance reviews by the National Stock Exchange and SEBI's own Integrated Surveillance Department. According to the firm's submissions, those reports found no evidence of manipulation in 48 out of 53 trading periods — more than 90% — and the Bank Nifty moved contrary to the alleged manipulative direction in the vast majority of them.

In 48 of 53 trading periods examined, NSE and SEBI's own surveillance reports found no evidence of manipulation — and the Bank Nifty moved against the alleged manipulative direction, Jane Street told the tribunal.

The firm also sought “full and free” inspection of the investigative materials behind SEBI's order: detailed correspondence between the regulator and the NSE, redacted counterparty information included in SEBI's trade logs, and the whistleblower complaints that prompted the regulator to set up its investigation committee. Withholding these documents, Khambata argued, would “vitiate” the process and breach the principles of natural justice.

SEBI's side: a renewed probe and rejected requests

The regulator's position is that its interim order stands on evidence of genuine abuse — and notably, SEBI renewed its investigation against Jane Street even after the NSE and the regulator's surveillance department had cleared the firm. Jane Street is now asking the tribunal to compel SEBI to hand over the trading and counterparty data backing the ₹4,843.58-crore enforcement order.

SEBI, for its part, has reportedly rejected requests for additional documents, telling the tribunal it would not share material it had not relied upon while passing its interim order. That refusal is precisely what the firm is challenging: if the regulator cannot show its full working, can the order stand? How the tribunal answers that question matters well beyond this one firm.

Why this case is bigger than Jane Street

There are three reasons this hearing belongs on every Indian trader's radar:

  • It is a test case for SEBI's authority. This is the regulator's biggest enforcement action against a sophisticated global participant in India's derivatives market. The outcome will define how far SEBI can go in policing expiry-day trading — and what evidence standard it must meet.
  • The backdrop is retail losses on a staggering scale. Indian regulators have tightened the screws on the booming equity options market precisely because individual investors have suffered widespread losses on the other side of trades done by sophisticated global entities — Bloomberg's reporting puts retail F&O losses at roughly $9.6 billion in the year ended March, consistent with SEBI's own August 2026 study, which found that 87.7% of individual F&O traders ended FY26 in net loss, with aggregate retail losses of ₹91,685 crore. This case is the enforcement face of that crackdown.
  • Expiry-day trading is under the microscope. If the tribunal ultimately upholds manipulation findings on expiry days, expect even closer surveillance of expiry sessions and possibly stricter participation norms — changes that would directly affect how retail options traders operate.

What Indian F&O traders should actually watch

You do not need a legal team to draw useful lessons from this case. Five things to keep in mind:

  • Treat violent expiry moves with scepticism, not FOMO. The entire case rests on the claim that index moves on expiry days were engineered. Whether or not the tribunal agrees, the lesson stands: sharp expiry-session swings are not always “just price action”.
  • Watch the disclosure fight. If SAT orders SEBI to share its trade logs and correspondence, future enforcement cases will have to be built on evidence the other side can actually see — a meaningful precedent for market fairness.
  • Do not trade the rumour. Cases like this generate a lot of noise. No tribunal argument changes where Nifty closes on a given day — stick to your plan and your position sizing. Our breakdown of SEBI's F&O study is a useful reminder of why discipline matters more than drama.
  • Expect the F&O rulebook to keep evolving. Win or lose for Jane Street, the regulator's scrutiny of derivatives trading is not going away. Stricter norms around expiry trading, margin and position limits are the direction of travel.
  • Regulated venues still matter. The case is a reminder of why trading on regulated exchanges through SEBI-registered intermediaries is non-negotiable — see our guide to choosing a safe broker.

What happens next

The next hearing was scheduled for 6 October 2026 — today. Watch for three things: whether the tribunal directs SEBI to share the requested documents, any interim directions on the ₹4,843-crore order, or simply a fresh hearing date. This case will run for some time — the SAT has not yet delivered anything approaching a final ruling, and both sides have dug in.

Jane Street's argument is that the market moved the other way. The regulator's is that nobody should be able to move it at all. Whichever view prevails, the verdict will be felt on every expiry Thursday.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Jane Street–SEBI case about?

In an interim order dated 3 July 2025, SEBI accused New York-based trading firm Jane Street of manipulating India's equity derivatives market through coordinated trading in cash and derivatives on expiry days, focused on the Bank Nifty (18 key trading days between January 2023 and March 2025) and the Nifty (three expiry days in May 2025). The regulator impounded about ₹4,843 crore of alleged unlawful gains. Jane Street denied the allegations and challenged the order before the Securities Appellate Tribunal.

What did Jane Street argue at the SAT hearing on 5 October 2026?

Jane Street's counsel argued that earlier surveillance reviews by the NSE and SEBI's own Integrated Surveillance Department had found no evidence of manipulation in 48 of 53 trading periods examined — over 90% — and that the Bank Nifty moved contrary to the alleged manipulative direction in the vast majority of cases. The firm sought full inspection of SEBI's investigative materials, arguing that withholding documents would breach principles of natural justice.

Has Jane Street resumed trading in India?

No. Jane Street deposited the ₹4,843 crore impounded by SEBI's interim order but has not resumed trading in Indian markets. The appeal before the Securities Appellate Tribunal continues, with hearings held on 5 October 2026 and 6 October 2026.
